# Prototype Fidelity

**Fidelity** is how closely a prototype resembles the eventual design. Houde and Hill define it as "closeness to the eventual design" and distinguish it from **resolution**, "amount of detail" (p. 3). Lim et al. keep the same distinction under different names: resolution is the "level of detail or sophistication of what is manifested", while **scope** is the range of the design the prototype covers (p. 7:11). The three terms are often collapsed into a single low-versus-high scale.

## Why the binary scale fails

The low/high distinction is useful for orienting a designer, but Lim et al. report that it is "sometimes problematic" (p. 7:5). A prototype can be high-resolution in one dimension and low in another; McCurdy et al.'s mixed-fidelity work, cited by Lim et al., combines the two on different dimensions of a design (p. 7:5). The scale also says nothing about which dimension is being explored, so two prototypes at the same "fidelity" can answer entirely different questions.

Houde and Hill make the same point from the design-process side: a finished-looking prototype may be made early, for market research, while a rough one may be made late, to emphasize structure over visual detail in a user test (p. 3). Degree of refinement "does not necessarily correspond to the solidity of the design, or to a particular stage in the process" (p. 3).

## What finish does to an audience

Finish is read as a claim about completeness, whether or not the designer intends it. Houde and Hill record a sketchy interactive story whose hand-drawn style was meant to keep discussion on overall features; members of the organization instead wanted the sketch style in the final product (p. 7). A twenty-minute animation of a fashion design workspace had to be introduced with careful explanations that the design was not implemented, because an audience seeing it on a computer screen assumed it worked (p. 9).

Erickson gives the complementary account of roughness. A rough prototype relaxes the conventions of its medium or shows evidence of how it was made, and that roughness "leaves openings for discussion of the design" (p. 12). Omitting a feature tends to produce more discussion than including it; Salomon's example, cited by Erickson, is a 3D-looking button that shifted a discussion from basic functionality to button styling (p. 12). Roughness also lowers commitment on both sides: the designer can discount a criticized idea, and users criticize something that is obviously provisional (p. 12). Erickson cites Black's study of graphic designers, where rough early paper sketches correlated with greater satisfaction with the final design (p. 13).

## What low fidelity costs

The evidence does not say lower is always better. In Lim et al.'s mobile-phone study, the paper prototype could not register button presses, so feedback was delayed and some screen images were ambiguous; the computer-screen prototype led users to click the screen and the laptop keyboard (pp. 7:21-22). Those are false findings: problems caused by the prototype rather than the design. The same study also found missing findings, where a prototype's limits hid a real problem that the fully functional phone revealed (p. 7:22). The authors' conclusion is not that fidelity should be maximized but that material, resolution, and scope must be chosen with their perceptual effects in mind (p. 7:22).

## Trade-offs

- Higher fidelity costs more and invites the audience to treat the design as settled; it can also divert effort from design into production (Erickson, pp. 10-11).
- Lower fidelity is cheaper and invites critique, but it can produce false and missing findings, and broad audiences may not take it seriously (Houde and Hill, p. 15; Lim et al., pp. 7:21-22).
- The right level depends on the question, the audience, and the material's effect on the quality being judged, not on a stage in the process (Houde and Hill, p. 3; Lim et al., p. 7:14).

## When not to apply

A rough prototype is a poor choice when the question itself is about polish, performance, or sensory quality that only the final material can reveal. Conversely, a high-fidelity prototype is a poor choice when the goal is to generate alternatives or invite criticism, because finish narrows the conversation. Where the audience is external or senior, the cost of polishing may be justified by the need to be believed, but Erickson warns that the same polish can turn the prototype into public relations and suppress realistic questions about failure (pp. 10-11).

## My Understanding

Fidelity is a property of the prototype, not of the design, and treating it as a progress indicator is the mistake both source pairs warn against. The useful discipline is to ask three separate questions: how much detail, how much of the design, and in what material. Only the first is fidelity in the narrow sense, and the other two often explain why two prototypes that look equally finished produce different feedback.
