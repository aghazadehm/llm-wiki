# Prototype

A **prototype** is "any representation of a design idea, regardless of medium" (Houde and Hill, p. 3). The definition is deliberately broad: a molded foam model, a paper storyboard, an interactive slide show, and a test program all qualify, and so does an existing object pressed into service to answer a design question. What makes something a prototype is not its material or finish but the use a designer makes of it.

## The problem it addresses

Interactive systems combine software, hardware, visual, auditory, and interactive features, and users experience the combined effect (Houde and Hill, p. 1). Designing such a system means answering many interdependent questions at once, and building the whole artifact to answer them is slow and expensive. A prototype narrows the question: it is a concrete representation that can be examined, demonstrated, and criticized before the product exists.

Lim, Stolterman, and Tenenberg add the cognitive reason this matters. Design ideas become available for reflection only when they are externalized, so that "the world can speak back to us" (p. 7:9, quoting Schön). The prototype is the external form that makes an idea discussable, both by the designer and by others.

## What counts as a prototype

Houde and Hill answer the boundary question with an example: "Is a brick a prototype? The answer depends on how it is used" (p. 2). Used to represent the weight and scale of a future artifact, the brick prototypes exactly those qualities. This is why the medium is a poor classifier. A paper storyboard can be a prototype while a polished 3D model may not be, depending on the question each is used to explore.

The same breadth applies to who makes one. Houde and Hill define a designer as anyone who creates a prototype in order to design, regardless of job title (p. 3). That matters on multidisciplinary teams, where the industrial designer, interaction designer, programmer, and user researcher may each recognize a different object as "the prototype" (p. 2).

## Prototype and product

A prototype is a means of design, not a smaller version of the product. Lim et al. put it directly: "Prototypes are means and tools for design and are not the ultimate target for design" (p. 7:14). Even a fully working prototype differs from the product in its purpose, its quality requirements, and the way it is allowed to change.

This distinction is easy to lose once a prototype works. Houde and Hill observe that implementation prototypes "actually work", so they commonly "find their way directly into the final system", carrying a temporary user interface and a structure built for demonstration rather than maintenance (p. 12). Floyd makes the same point about experimental prototypes: retaining an unsuitable prototype as the production system defeats the purpose of building it as a learning vehicle (p. 9).

## Costs and failure modes

- **A prototype is not self-explanatory.** Audiences read finish as completeness, and the designer has to say what the prototype does and does not address (Houde and Hill, pp. 2 and 15).
- **The wrong prototype answers the wrong question.** A prototype built to explore look and feel will not settle a performance question, and the effort spent on it is not transferable (Houde and Hill, pp. 4-6).
- **Working prototypes drift into production.** The temporary interface and demonstration-quality code can survive into release (Houde and Hill, p. 12).
- **A prototype can create commitment.** Once users have evaluated a demonstrated prototype, changing it without their consent creates acceptance problems (Floyd, p. 5).

## When not to apply

Prototyping is not free, and Floyd sets conditions on when exploratory prototyping is worth its cost: tools must keep construction cheap, and the expected lifetime or quality requirements of the system must justify the investment (p. 7). Where the design question is already settled by analysis or by an existing product, a prototype adds cost without adding knowledge. A prototype also does not by itself secure user participation or protect users' interests; Floyd warns that prototyping can be misused to give the appearance of participation while tuning the system against users' interests (p. 15).

## My Understanding

The definition earns its breadth by moving the question from "what is this object?" to "what is this object for?". Once that shift is made, arguments about paper versus code, or low versus high fidelity, stop being about legitimacy and become about fit: a pizza box can be the right prototype for a weight question, and a polished video can be the right prototype for an audience that needs to believe the future is real. The risk is the same shift seen from the other side: an object that looks like a product invites the audience to judge it as one.
