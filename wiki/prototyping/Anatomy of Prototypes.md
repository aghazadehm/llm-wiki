# Anatomy of Prototypes

The **anatomy of prototypes** is Lim, Stolterman, and Tenenberg's framework for describing any prototype along two sets of dimensions: what it **filters** from a design space, and how it **manifests** the design idea (p. 7:11). It is descriptive rather than prescriptive: the authors offer it as a language for analyzing and planning prototypes, not as a method that produces good ones (pp. 7:10 and 7:24).

## Two principles

The framework rests on a **fundamental prototyping principle**: "Prototyping is an activity with the purpose of creating a manifestation that, in its simplest form, filters the qualities in which designers are interested, without distorting the understanding of the whole" (p. 7:4). The wording makes incompleteness the point. A prototype is useful "as much in what [it does] not include as in what [it does]" (p. 7:7), because leaving something out defers a decision and keeps the question narrow.

An **economic principle** governs the choice of how much to build: "The best prototype is one that, in the simplest and most efficient way, makes the possibilities and limitations of a design idea visible and measurable" (p. 7:4).

## Filtering dimensions

Filtering dimensions name the aspects of a design idea a prototype can isolate (Table II, p. 7:11):

- **Appearance** — form, color, texture, size, weight, shape, and their proportions.
- **Data** — information architecture and the data model: type, size, organization, labeling, privacy.
- **Functionality** — the functions the design can perform and the scenarios in which they are used.
- **Interactivity** — input, output, feedback, and operation behavior.
- **Spatial structure** — how components are arranged and related, in two or three dimensions, tangible or intangible.

The list is not claimed to be complete, only useful (p. 7:13). The dimensions are also intertwined: the iPod's wheel interface is an interactivity decision that drove its appearance, because a device holding more than 200 songs could not browse them with buttons (p. 7:13). Working on one dimension separately is still necessary to settle its details (p. 7:13).

## Manifestation dimensions

Manifestation dimensions describe how the filtered aspect is made concrete (Table III, p. 7:11):

- **Material** — the medium used to form the prototype, visible or invisible: paper, foam, code, a toolkit, or an existing object made to behave in a certain way (p. 7:11).
- **Resolution** — the level of detail or sophistication, corresponding to fidelity (p. 7:11).
- **Scope** — how much of the design the prototype covers, independent of how detailed it is (p. 7:14).

Scope is the dimension most easily confused with resolution. A color-scheme test can use color charts alone (narrow scope) or a full page layout (broad scope) at either level of detail (p. 7:14). The authors treat the three as a set because the choice of one constrains the others: paper is cheap until the prototype must communicate detailed interaction, at which point the material becomes expensive (p. 7:19).

## Prototype profiles and the evidence

A **prototype profile** records the dimensions a prototype addresses and the values chosen for material, resolution, and scope (p. 7:15). The framework is illustrated by two case studies.

In the first, the same house design was shown as a 2D blueprint and as a 3D virtual model. The blueprint filtered spatial structure; the 3D model filtered appearance, interactivity, and spatial structure (Table IV, p. 7:17). Users responded in kind: the blueprint group talked about layout and remodeling, while the 3D group talked about how the house felt and misremembered the layout badly (pp. 7:17-18).

In the second, the same text-messaging feature was tested through a paper prototype, a computer-screen prototype, and a fully functional phone. All three targeted the same filtering dimension, interactivity, but differed in material, resolution, and scope (Table V, p. 7:21). Only about twenty percent of the usability findings were common to all three; the rest were either false findings caused by the prototype or findings the prototype could not reveal (pp. 7:21-22).

## Trade-offs

- Filtering is a strength and a limit: a prototype cannot inform about a dimension it does not manifest (p. 7:18).
- Adding dimensions makes a prototype more complete but harder to interpret, so the designer has to choose deliberately (p. 7:18).
- Material changes perception. The same color question answered with a virtual model and a life-size foam model produces different reactions (p. 7:14).
- The framework costs time to apply; the authors present it as a reflective and analytic tool, not a shortcut (p. 7:24).

## When not to apply

The anatomy does not tell a designer which prototype to build. The authors say the notion of a good prototype "can only be understood in relation to the specific purpose of the design process and to the specific issue that a designer is trying to explore" (p. 7:24). Where the purpose is already precise and the team is experienced, a full profile may be overhead; where several prototypes must be compared or handed between teams, it gives them a shared vocabulary.

## My Understanding

The most useful move is the separation of "what am I filtering?" from "how am I manifesting it?". Fidelity debates usually collapse the two, so a team argues about paper versus code when the real question is whether the material distorts the quality being judged. The twenty-percent result is the empirical version of that argument: three prototypes of the same feature produced mostly different findings, and the differences were not noise but artifacts of the prototypes themselves.
