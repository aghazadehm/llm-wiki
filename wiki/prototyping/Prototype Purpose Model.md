# Prototype Purpose Model

The **prototype purpose model** is Houde and Hill's three-dimensional account of what a prototype can prototype: **role**, **look and feel**, and **implementation** (p. 3). Each corner stands for a class of design questions, and a prototype's position in the triangle says which class it addresses and, just as importantly, which it leaves alone.

## The three dimensions

- **Role** asks what function the artifact serves in a user's life, "the way in which it is useful to them" (p. 3).
- **Look and feel** asks about "the concrete sensory experience of using an artifact", what the user looks at, feels, and hears (p. 3).
- **Implementation** asks about "the techniques and components through which an artifact performs its function", the nuts and bolts of how it works (p. 3).

The triangle is drawn askew "to emphasize that no one dimension is inherently more important than any other" (p. 3). The model is relative and subjective: a position near a corner means that, in the designer's judgment, more attention went to that class of question than to the other two (p. 3).

## Markers and negative space

A marker on the triangle is a communication device. It gives the designer and the audience "a global sense of what the prototype is intended to explore; and equally important, what it does not explore" (p. 3). That second half is the model's practical contribution: it makes the prototype's omissions explicit before an audience reads them as failures.

The model also yields four working categories. Role, look-and-feel, and implementation prototypes sit near their corners; **integration prototypes** occupy the middle, exploring a balance of all three (p. 6). Integration prototypes are the closest to the final experience and the most expensive, because they may need to be as complex as the product (p. 12).

## What the examples show

Houde and Hill draw fifteen examples from real projects, and the 3D space-planning project is the clearest demonstration that the dimensions are independent. Three prototypes were built almost in parallel: an interactive slide show that conveyed the proposed role, a Macromedia Director prototype that tested a handle-box interaction for moving furniture, and a working renderer that explored how much 3D performance the target machine could deliver (pp. 4-5). No single prototype represented the design at that stage; the design existed mainly as "a shared concept in the minds of the designers" (p. 5). Keeping the investigations separate let each question be answered clearly, and the answers constrained one another before being integrated (pp. 5-6).

The examples also show how audience changes the required resolution without changing the dimension. A sketchy interactive story worked for the design team but led parts of the organization to fall in love with the sketch style (p. 7). The Knowledge Navigator video was produced to a much higher finish because its audience was broad and needed to visualize the future concretely (pp. 7-8). In both cases the prototype was a role prototype; only the audience differed.

## Trade-offs

- The model is a lens, not a sequence. The space-planning prototypes were built in parallel, not in order of increasing detail (p. 5).
- A prototype can address more than one dimension, but adding dimensions makes it more complex and harder to interpret (Lim et al., p. 7:18).
- The marker records the designer's intention, not the audience's reading. A prototype presented without preparation will be judged on whatever dimension its appearance suggests (Houde and Hill, p. 15).

## When not to apply

The model is built for interactive artifacts, and its three dimensions are a starting vocabulary rather than a complete ontology. Lim et al. later propose a finer set of filtering dimensions that includes data and spatial structure, which the triangle does not name separately (p. 7:11). Where a prototype's purpose is already obvious to its audience, the marker adds little; where the audience is broad or unfamiliar with design practice, it is the main defense against misreading.

## My Understanding

The model is most useful read backwards: instead of asking "what kind of prototype should we build?", ask "which question are we unable to answer?", then build the smallest thing that answers it. The 3D space-planning project is convincing because the three prototypes were not drafts of one another; they were three different inquiries whose results had to be reconciled later. That is a more honest picture of early design than the usual story of increasing fidelity.
