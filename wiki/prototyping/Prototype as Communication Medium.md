# Prototype as Communication Medium

**Prototypes as communication media** is Thomas Erickson's account of design as a distributed social process in which concrete artifacts mediate talk among the people who design and use a product, and between them and the organization around them (p. 2). On this view a prototype is judged not only by what it demonstrates but by the conversation it makes possible, and different audiences need different artifacts or different uses of the same one.

## Audiences and stages

Erickson distinguishes three audiences: the **design team**, whose members come from different disciplines and may not share a language; the **users**, who know their domain but not the technology; and the **organization**, which includes executives, managers, and future implementors and which decides whether the design proceeds (pp. 3-4). He also distinguishes three overlapping stages: **exploration**, where the problem is set and the team forms; **refinement**, where prototypes carry the details; and **transition**, where the design is evangelized and handed to implementors (pp. 3-4).

The pairing matters because the same artifact can be right for one audience and wrong for another. A rough role prototype may work for a design team and fail with a supporting organization, a point Houde and Hill make independently (p. 15).

## Vision prototypes

A **vision prototype** belongs to exploration. It is "some sort of concrete representation with an embedded story or scenario that shows actions at a very high level" (p. 10). Its job is to communicate the gist: what the design is, how it helps people with things they care about, and where it fits in the flow of a day (p. 10). The usual audience is the organization, and the usual form is a video or a partially interactive demonstration with hand-drawn scenes and a soundtrack (p. 10).

A vision prototype has to be convincing enough that the audience believes the depiction of use, and obviously a concept rather than a product about to ship (p. 10). When it is polished for a broad or senior audience, the cost rises and so does the risk: energy moves from design to production, and realistic questions such as what happens when something goes wrong may be avoided (pp. 10-11). Erickson's Knowledge Navigator example shows the other side, where a polished public prototype stimulated a debate about the technology's social consequences (p. 11).

## Working prototypes

A **working prototype** belongs to refinement. It embodies the current design and serves as a medium for interaction among designers and for reflection by an individual designer (p. 11). Erickson gives it two properties:

- **Accessibility** — any team member should be able to modify it. HyperCard and Director come closest among software tools, because a graphic designer can change graphics, an interaction designer can adjust dialogs, and a programmer can add functionality; more powerful environments tend to be accessible only to programmers (pp. 11-12). Physical mock-ups are the alternative when the goal is to embody general ideas quickly (p. 12).
- **Roughness** — the prototype should look provisional. Roughness creates ambiguity that different designers resolve differently, leaves openings for discussion, and lowers the commitment of both designer and user (pp. 12-13). A rough prototype "has built-in deniability" (p. 12).

## Stories as the complementary artifact

Erickson pairs prototypes with stories. Stories are concrete accounts of particular people and events, while scenarios are more abstract scripts that leave out history, motivation, and personality (pp. 5-6). Stories are useful at the start because they are ambiguous enough to support several interpretations, and because collecting them builds a shared language on a multidisciplinary team (p. 6). Design stories, such as the "guilt pile", carry rationale that a list of requirements cannot: they describe not only actions but feelings and motivations (p. 7). Stories are memorable and informal, which suits knowledge that is uncertain and local, and they are used most in exploration and transition, while prototypes dominate refinement (pp. 8-9).

## Trade-offs

- A polished prototype can persuade, but it can also substitute persuasion for design and suppress questions about failure (pp. 10-11).
- A rough prototype invites critique, but it can be dismissed by audiences who equate finish with seriousness (Houde and Hill, p. 15).
- Accessibility and power trade off: the environments anyone can edit are usually the ones that cannot implement every interaction (p. 12).
- Stories are vivid but unreliable as evidence; Erickson calls them "layers of gloss accreted around an irritating grain of reality" and recommends combining them with observation and interviews (p. 9).

## When not to apply

The communication view does not replace empirical work. Erickson explicitly says stories should not be the only source of information and pairs them with observation, interviews, ethnography, and user participation (p. 9). A prototype built mainly to communicate can also mislead if the audience is not prepared for what it omits, which is the same failure Houde and Hill describe. Where the design question is technical and the audience is a small expert team, the communication framing adds little that the engineering artifact does not already provide.

## My Understanding

The article's most useful claim is that a prototype's quality is relational: it depends on who is looking and what they need to do next. That explains why the same rough sketch can be excellent for a design team and damaging in front of executives, and why polishing is not simply an upgrade. It also explains the persistent gap between a prototype that demonstrates a feature and a prototype that starts a useful argument about whether the feature is worth having.
