# What Do Prototypes Prototype?

This chapter by Stephanie Houde and Charles Hill, both at Apple Computer, appeared in the second edition of the *Handbook of Human-Computer Interaction* (Elsevier, 1997). It argues that the vocabulary designers use for prototypes, tool names and fidelity labels, obscures the more useful question of what a prototype is meant to explore, and proposes a three-cornered model to replace it.

## What it says

The chapter opens with the difficulty of talking about prototypes on a multidisciplinary team. Industrial designers, interaction designers, programmers, and user-studies specialists each call a different object a prototype, and organizations develop "prototyping cultures" that recognize only some forms as valid (p. 2). Houde and Hill define an artifact as the interactive system being designed, a prototype as any representation of a design idea regardless of medium, and a designer as anyone who creates a prototype in order to design (p. 3).

The model's dimensions are role, look and feel, and implementation, each standing for a class of design questions (p. 3). A marker placed in the triangle records the designer's judgment about which class received most attention, and therefore what the prototype does not address (p. 3). The chapter groups fifteen examples from real projects into role, look-and-feel, implementation, and integration prototypes (pp. 6-14). The 3D space-planning project shows three prototypes built in parallel rather than in order of increasing detail (pp. 4-6); the Knowledge Navigator and Integrated Communicator examples show how a broad audience can require high resolution even when the question is role (pp. 7-9); the pizza-box and GloBall examples show how little construction a useful look-and-feel prototype may need (pp. 9-10).

The closing recommendations are to define prototype broadly, build multiple focused prototypes, know the audience, and prepare the audience by saying what the prototype does and does not explore (p. 15).

## My Understanding

The chapter is a terminology intervention more than a technique catalogue. Its examples are memorable because each one makes the same point in a different medium: the brick, the pizza box, the sketchy story, and the polished video are all prototypes when they answer a design question, and none of them is more legitimate than the others for that reason alone. The weak point is the marker: it records intention, but the chapter shows repeatedly that audiences read finish instead.
