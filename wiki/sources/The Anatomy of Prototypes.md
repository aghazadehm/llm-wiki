# The Anatomy of Prototypes

This article by Youn-Kyung Lim, Erik Stolterman, and Josh Tenenberg appeared in *ACM Transactions on Computer-Human Interaction* 15(2), Article 7, in July 2008. It argues that research on prototyping has focused on techniques and evaluation while lacking a fundamental account of what a prototype is, and offers an "anatomy" of two dimension sets as that account.

## What it says

The authors begin by criticizing the dominance of fidelity in prototyping discourse and the treatment of prototypes as evaluation tools (pp. 7:2, 7:4-7:5). They cite mixed-fidelity work and their own studies to show that the low/high distinction is too coarse (p. 7:5). Their alternative treats prototypes as filters that traverse a design space and as manifestations of design ideas (p. 7:4).

The framework has two principles. The fundamental principle says prototyping creates a manifestation that, in its simplest form, filters the qualities of interest without distorting the whole (p. 7:4). The economic principle says the best prototype makes a design idea's possibilities and limitations visible and measurable in the simplest and most efficient way (p. 7:4).

The filtering dimensions are appearance, data, functionality, interactivity, and spatial structure (Table II, p. 7:11). The manifestation dimensions are material, resolution, and scope (Table III, p. 7:11). A prototype profile records the values chosen for a particular prototype (p. 7:15). Two case studies supply the evidence: a house shown as a blueprint and as a 3D model (pp. 7:16-7:18), and a mobile-phone text-messaging feature shown as paper, computer-screen, and fully functional prototypes, where only about twenty percent of usability findings were shared (pp. 7:18-7:22).

The authors present the anatomy as descriptive and reflective rather than prescriptive, and list four broad purposes for prototypes: evaluation and testing, understanding user experience, idea generation, and communication among designers (pp. 7:10, 7:24).

## My Understanding

The article is strongest where it turns a slogan into a measurable claim: incompleteness is not a compromise but the mechanism by which a prototype isolates a quality. The case studies are what make it credible, because they show that changing the manifestation while holding the filtering dimension constant changes the findings. The framework's weakness is its size: five filtering dimensions and three manifestation dimensions are a lot to hold in mind, and the authors admit the list is not complete.
