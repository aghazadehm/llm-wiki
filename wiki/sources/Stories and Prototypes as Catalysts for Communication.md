# Stories and Prototypes as Catalysts for Communication

Thomas Erickson wrote this chapter, whose full title is "Notes on Design Practice: Stories and Prototypes as Catalysts for Communication", while at Apple Computer. A version appeared in *Scenario-Based Design: Envisioning Work and Technology in System Development*, edited by John Carroll (Wiley, 1995). It treats design as a social process and asks what makes stories and prototypes effective at moving knowledge between the people involved.

## What it says

Erickson sets out six problems of design practice: problem setting, team building, involving users, collaborative design, design transfer, and design evangelism (pp. 1-2). He then models design as communication among three audiences, the design team, users, and the organization, across three overlapping stages: exploration, refinement, and transition (pp. 3-4). Design artifacts are the material or informational objects that mediate this communication (p. 2).

Stories are the artifact of exploration and transition. They differ from scenarios in being concrete, personal, and often about atypical events (pp. 5-6). Collecting users' stories helps the team understand a usage domain and build a shared language; design stories such as the "guilt pile" carry rationale and motivation (pp. 6-7). Stories are memorable and informal, which makes them effective with senior management and in design transfer, but they are not reliable evidence on their own (pp. 8-9).

Prototypes are the artifact of refinement. Erickson distinguishes vision prototypes, which communicate the gist of a design to the organization, from working prototypes, which embody the design for the team (pp. 9-11). Working prototypes are most effective when they are accessible to every discipline on the team and rough enough to invite modification and critique (pp. 11-13).

## My Understanding

The chapter's contribution is to make the audience a first-class design consideration. The same prototype can be a success with a design team and a failure with executives, and Erickson explains why without treating either audience as irrational. The pairing with stories is also useful: stories carry the why, prototypes carry the what, and a team that has only one of them tends to lose either the rationale or the detail.
